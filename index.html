Cue ball control and table strategy

Mindset Break Cue ball action Angles Planning Drills

Mindset and routine

  • Practice is tuning; playing is running the program. Fix stroke, elbow and stance in practice. In a match, do what feels comfortable on the day - forcing it means playing badly, getting frustrated and not winning.
  • Comfort shifts as you practise. What feels natural now is not what felt natural two weeks or three months ago. Let it evolve.
  • Pre-shot routine on every shot. Never get lazy on it.
  • It is certainty, not mechanics. When confident, the stroke happens naturally. Doubt after a bad run is what makes you misread angles you would normally see.
  • Trust the line. Adjusting the aim once you are down is a trust problem. If it feels wrong, stand up and get down again.
  • Check the breath. It changes everything.

The break (7-foot table)

  • Accuracy over power. 9-foot needs power; 7-foot does not. An accurate break is usually already very good.
  • Hit the cue ball square. Mine drifted off to the right.
  • Not too low. If the cue ball draws back off the rack, the strike was below centre.
  • Find your own power source. Some players use the wrist - it works if it is yours. An open bridge is fine.
  • Straight-stroke check: after the follow-through, notice which part of your hand touches the table. Landing on the same spot each time means you stayed straight.

Choosing the cue ball action

Followeasier thanStuneasier thanDraw
  • If you can get there with follow, follow. It is the most repeatable.
  • Avoid in-between shots (half stop, half follow). They are hard to repeat on the same line.
  • Choose for your own strengths. Good at draw: smooth one-rail draw. Not: follow with a little side and play the short side.
  • Side as insurance. A touch of side (for example draw with a bit of right) gets you there even when the main action comes up short.
  • The soft stop - tip a bit lower, hit softer, so the cue ball still slides. Pace plus feel; only repetition teaches it.
  • Topspin adds speed. A rolling cue ball keeps accelerating after contact, so it needs less cue speed than you think.

Reading angles

  • Thickness first, then the line. You cannot decide pace or path until you know the cut.

  • Tangent line: stun sends the cue ball off at 90°; a rolling ball follows the natural angle forward.
  • Near-straight shots need more pace than you think. A 5° cut is almost straight - cheat the pocket slightly or hit firmer.
  • Object ball close to the pocket: pot precisely. Entering the left or right side of the pocket can change the cue ball path by 10°.
  • Know, do not guess. Learn what the rails and side do. Players who guess are unreliable; players who know what the cue ball will do clear frames consistently.

Planning the table

  • Shot by shot, explore the options: follow, then draw, then stop, and in-between last.
  • Play for the natural angle on the next ball, so you can follow through and remove problem balls.
  • When unsure, err long. Too much leaves a longer shot; too little gets you stuck behind a ball.
  • Deal with problem balls early while the table is open. Take the trickiest ball when it is convenient, and do not play into clusters that push balls into bad spots.
  • The black still needs cue ball thought. A scratch loses the frame. Rolling guarantees no scratch; stun can scratch.
  • English bridge at an angle - far more stable than a makeshift bridge on awkward shots.

Practice drills

  • The soft stop / slide shot - same pot, vary the pace.
  • One-rail draw back at controlled pace.
  • Follow with near-maximum side - learn how much side takes you where off the first rail.
  • Breaks focused on a square, centre-ball hit rather than power.
